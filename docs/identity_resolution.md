# Identity resolution

How players from two providers are recognised as the same person, and why the
system is built to prefer missing a match over making a wrong one.

Specification section 6 governs this. The current measured results live in
`docs/identity_resolution_report.md`, which is regenerated by
`python -m pipelines.identity_resolution.run` — this document explains the
method; that one reports what it did.

---

## The asymmetry that shapes everything

A missed match leaves a player absent from the site, and visibly so.

A wrong match silently attaches one person's statistics to another's profile.
Every figure derived from that player — percentiles, role scores, similarity,
recruitment rankings — is then wrong, and wrong in a way nothing downstream can
detect. It looks exactly like a correct profile.

The two errors are not equally bad, so the system does not treat them as such.
Thresholds are set to protect precision at the cost of recall, and everything
below the threshold goes to a person rather than being resolved automatically.

---

## Never on name alone

Section 6 is explicit, and the implementation enforces it: a name is one signal
among several and cannot reach the auto-match threshold on its own, whatever its
similarity.

Football makes this concrete. Names repeat heavily within and across countries,
transliteration varies by source, and the same player appears as a full legal
name in one dataset and a two-word playing name in another. Any of those, matched
on name alone, produces a confident wrong answer.

Signals used together:

- normalised name
- date of birth
- nationality
- current club
- position

---

## Name normalisation

Before comparison, names are folded to a common form: lowercased, accents
stripped, punctuation removed, whitespace collapsed.

### The transliteration table, and why it exists

Unicode decomposition handles accented letters — `é` becomes `e`. It does not
handle letters where the stroke *is* the letter rather than a mark on it: `ø`,
`đ`, `ł`, `ß`, `æ`, `þ`, and the dotless `ı`. Those survive decomposition
unchanged and are then stripped by the punctuation pass, so `Håland` normalises
correctly while `Ødegaard` loses its first character entirely.

Measured against the real Transfermarkt set, this mangled **516 of 50,149
names** — roughly 1% — and every one of them was a player from Scandinavia,
Poland, the Balkans, Germany, Turkey or Iceland. The failure was not random; it
fell entirely on particular nationalities.

An explicit transliteration table now folds those letters before decomposition.

## Comparing names

A plain string-similarity ratio was not good enough. Two real failures drove the
current implementation:

- `Vylius` against `Vilius` — the same player, one character apart, scoring 0.69
- `L. Farrugia` against `Liam Farrugia` — an initial against a full first name,
  also 0.69

Both had an **exact date of birth match**, and both were being rejected.

The current comparison aligns tokens greedily rather than comparing whole
strings: each token in one name is paired with its best counterpart in the
other, an initial matches a first name beginning with that letter, and
near-identical tokens score highly rather than being penalised for a single
character.

**Measured effect:** recall rose from 67.9% to 86.0% with precision unchanged at
100%.

## The confidence ladder

Section 6 defines the shape; the implementation follows it:

| Confidence | Basis |
| --- | --- |
| 1.00 | Exact normalised name + exact date of birth + compatible club |
| 0.98 | Exact date of birth + very strong name similarity |
| 0.95 | Exact normalised name + date of birth |
| 0.90 | Date of birth + nationality + strong name similarity + club |

**Auto-match threshold: 0.90.** Below it, nothing is matched automatically.

**Margin: 0.05.** A candidate must beat the runner-up by this much. Two players
who both score 0.94 are ambiguous, not a match — picking the higher one would
be arbitrary, and arbitrary is how careers get merged.

---

## Blocking

Comparing every FootyStats player against every Transfermarkt player is
quadratic and unnecessary. Candidates are restricted first by date of birth, or
by surname plus birth year where the date is absent.

Blocking is a performance measure and must not change the answer: a pair that
would match must not be excluded from consideration. Date of birth is safe
because it is also a required signal for every rung of the ladder above the
threshold.

---

## Manual overrides

Every mapping stores its method, its confidence, and whether a person confirmed
it. A manually confirmed mapping is flagged so that an automated re-run cannot
quietly overwrite a human decision — the point of asking a person is lost if the
next pipeline run discards their answer.

Low-confidence and ambiguous pairs are reported for review rather than resolved.

## How it was measured

There is no ground-truth mapping between FootyStats and Transfermarkt, and none
can exist before FootyStats does. So the evaluation uses a **shadow source**:
real Transfermarkt records, perturbed in the ways a second provider realistically
differs — abbreviated first names, transliteration differences, missing dates of
birth, stale clubs — for which the correct answer is known by construction.

Two runs, measuring the two failure modes separately:

1. **Mock performance players against Transfermarkt.** These names are invented,
   so the correct answer for every one is *no match*. Anything matched is a false
   positive.
2. **The perturbed shadow source against Transfermarkt.** Every record has a
   known correct counterpart, so anything unmatched is a false negative.

Current results are in `docs/identity_resolution_report.md`.

## What this does not yet do

FootyStats identities have never been matched, because FootyStats has never been
called. The framework is provider-independent and tested against a stand-in;
specification Phase 15 runs it for real, and cannot begin until Phase 12 and 13
have.
